Research is usually evaluated from the outside. Is the question important? Is the
methodology sound? Will the paper be accepted? Will anyone cite it? Will the work
have impact?

Those are all legitimate questions, and most of the machinery around us is built to
ask them. But there is another question that almost never gets asked out loud:

_**"Do I have a reason to continue doing this research?"**_

That is not the same as asking whether the research is good. A project can be
scientifically sound and still not be worth *your* time, attention, and intellectual
energy. A project that looks niche or uncertain from the outside can be deeply worth
pursuing to the person doing it. The two judgments run on different axes, and
confusing them is how people end up years into work they cannot explain to
themselves.

So the four questions below are not a measure of the quality or the importance of a
research project. They are a way of examining the relationship between the
researcher and the research. They ask whether the project is sustained by something
deeper than recognition, external pressure, habit, or attachment to one's own ideas.

This is my own way of thinking about research, not a prescription. You may well have
a different view, or the opposite one. If these questions help you sort out what you
think about a project you are holding, I am glad. If you find they cut against your
own values, I am equally glad, because that probably means you already know what is
driving you and are willing to hold your ground.

## 1. The Other

> **Can I name at least one person, group, or future reader whose life,
> understanding, or ability to act could genuinely be improved by this work?**

Research does not need broad societal impact to be worth doing. A theoretical result
may matter to a small community. A methodological improvement may help a handful of
people. An argument may do nothing more than let one reader see a problem
differently. What matters is that the work points somewhere beyond the person doing
it.

It is easy to justify a project by saying that you find it interesting. Curiosity is
a perfectly good reason to *begin*. But a project that demands months or years of
sustained attention deserves a harder question: is there anyone on the other side of
it for whom the result could matter? Perhaps someone will use the method. Perhaps
someone will understand a problem more clearly. Perhaps someone will make a better
decision, or challenge an assumption, or see a possibility that was invisible to them
before.

The point is not to predict impact. Research is uncertain, and we rarely know in
advance who ends up benefiting from what. The point is whether you can genuinely
imagine an Other for whom this work matters.

If the answer is completely empty, that is worth noticing. It may mean the project is
serving your curiosity, your career, your identity, or your wish to have produced
something, rather than addressing a problem you believe deserves to be addressed.
None of those motives is shameful. They are just not the same as having a reason.

## 2. The Silence

> **If nobody read, cited, praised, or recognized this research for the foreseeable
> future, would I still want to pursue the question?**

Research lives inside a powerful system of external recognition. We publish papers.
We present at conferences. We collect citations, reviews, awards, grants, promotions,
invitations, and the reactions of colleagues. None of this is bad. It is part of how
research communities coordinate and how work finds the people who need it.

But recognition can quietly substitute itself for the reason you started.

So imagine removing all of it. Nobody reads the paper. Nobody cites it. Nobody
praises it. Nobody knows you did it. Would you still want to understand the question?

This is not a test of whether you can enjoy working in isolation, and answering "no"
does not make the research worthless. A great deal of research is legitimately
instrumental. You might be solving a problem for a team, building expertise you will
need for something else, or working toward a practical goal that has nothing to do
with the pleasure of the question. That is fine, and it is worth knowing that it is
what you are doing.

What the question exposes is what is holding the commitment up. There is a real
difference between wanting the research to exist and wanting people to know that you
did the research. The two can coexist, and usually do. They are not the same thing,
and only one of them survives a long stretch with no external signal. The Silence
asks what is left when the signal is gone.

## 3. The Gaze

> **If someone told me to stop, could I distinguish their reasons from my desire for
> their approval or my fear of their judgment?**

Researchers are constantly exposed to other people's verdicts. This has already been
done. Nobody cares about this. This will not get published. You should work on
something else.

Sometimes they are right, and listening saves enormous amounts of time. Criticism
from people who know the field is one of the cheapest ways to avoid a wasted year.

But there is another possibility, which is that you stop not because their reasoning
convinced you, but because their judgment changed how you see yourself and your work.
The difference is invisible from the outside. Both look like a project quietly
ending.

So the useful question is not whether you would ignore them. Ignoring people rewards
stubbornness and nothing else. The question is whether you can hold your own judgment
in the presence of someone else's. You should be capable of saying that they have
convinced you and you are going to stop. You should also be capable of saying that
you understand why they think you should stop and you do not agree. Both sentences
need to be available to you. If only one of them is, the choice is being made
somewhere other than in your own head.

The goal is neither obedience nor rebellion. It is independence of judgment. A
project should not continue merely because you are too attached to abandon it, and it
should not end merely because someone whose opinion you value has decided it is not
worthwhile. The Gaze asks whether your commitment belongs to you, or whether it runs
on the permission of others.

## 4. The Opponent

> **When someone presents a serious argument against my position, do I genuinely want
> to find out whether they are right?**

This one is the hardest.

Suppose your research argues for A, and someone makes a serious case for B. There are
two very different first reactions. The first is *how do I refute this*. The second is
*what if they are right*.

The second demands a different kind of intellectual commitment. It means being
willing to investigate an argument that could undermine something you have spent
months or years building. It means reading carefully rather than defensively,
checking the evidence, examining your own assumptions, and accepting that your
position might not survive the exercise.

Notice that the question is not whether you *will* change your mind. It is whether you
*want to know*. Strong convictions are entirely compatible with intellectual openness.
In fact one of the strongest forms of commitment a person can have to a question
sounds like this: I care about this enough that I want the truth even if the answer
destroys my current position. That is what separates conviction from attachment.

If you cannot imagine genuinely wanting to discover that you are wrong, you may no
longer be investigating a question. You may be defending an identity, a conclusion,
or a piece of work that has become part of how you see yourself.

## What the Four Questions Reveal

The four are not independent. Each guards against a different way a project can drift
away from genuine intellectual motivation.

- **The Other**: does this research matter to anyone beyond myself?
- **The Silence**: would I still do it without recognition?
- **The Gaze**: can I hold my own judgment against external judgment?
- **The Opponent**: am I willing to discover that my judgment is wrong?

Together they point at four qualities: responsibility toward others, intrinsic
commitment, independence of judgment, and intellectual humility.

There is a tension between the last two, and it is worth stating plainly.
Independence without openness turns into stubbornness. Openness without independence
turns into intellectual dependence. Neither failure mode announces itself. What you
want is the narrow thing in between: independent enough not to hand your judgment
over to other people, open enough to let a good argument change it.

## What These Questions Are Not

They are not a scoring system. Four yes answers do not license a project, and two no
answers do not condemn one.

They also do not tell you whether the research is objectively valuable. A project can
be scientifically important even if you personally would not continue it without
external recognition. A project can be deeply meaningful to you and have almost no
external impact. Neither situation settles whether the work is good.

The purpose is narrower. These questions are meant to surface *why you are continuing*,
and whether that reason is one you would still endorse if you looked at it directly.

The most useful answer may not even be yes or no. It may be the discomfort you feel
when you notice that you cannot answer one of them honestly. That discomfort is
information, and before deciding whether to start, continue, or abandon a research
project, it is information worth having.
